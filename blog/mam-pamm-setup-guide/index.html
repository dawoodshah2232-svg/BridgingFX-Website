MAM and PAMM Setup Guide: Money Management Without the Headaches

Allocation methods, fee structures, risk controls, and the operational pitfalls — everything a broker needs to run money management properly.

BridgingFX editorial · 2026-06-20

Money management — MAM and PAMM structures that let skilled traders manage pooled client capital — is one of the highest-margin products a brokerage can offer, and one of the most operationally demanding. Done right, it attracts serious capital, generates performance-fee revenue, and differentiates you from every standard retail offering. Done wrong, it's a support nightmare wrapped in a compliance risk. Here's how to set it up properly.

MAM vs PAMM: which structure fits

PAMM (Percentage Allocation Management Module) pools investor funds into one account the manager trades; profits, losses, and fees are allocated by percentage of participation. It's simple, transparent, and easy for investors to understand — the classic pooled model.

MAM (Multi-Account Manager) keeps investor funds in separate sub-accounts, with the manager's trades replicated across them by lot allocation. It offers more flexibility — different leverage or risk multipliers per investor — at the cost of operational complexity. For most brokers starting out, PAMM's simplicity wins; MAM earns its complexity when you serve sophisticated allocators who want customization.

Whichever you choose, the technology must handle the unglamorous mechanics flawlessly: synchronized execution across sub-accounts, correct lot sizing on partial fills, and fee calculations that reconcile to the cent. Investors forgive a losing month; they never forgive a statement that doesn't add up.

Allocation and fee mechanics that don't create disputes

The two decisions that generate 90% of money-management disputes: how trades are allocated, and how fees are calculated. Allocation by equity percentage is the standard — but define exactly what happens with deposits and withdrawals mid-period, how partial closes are handled, and what the manager's own capital requirement is.

For fees, high-water marks aren't optional — they're the difference between a fair product and a fee machine. Performance fees should apply only to new profits above the previous peak, with crystal-clear calculation periods. Publish worked examples. The managers who attract real capital choose platforms where the fee math is transparent, because their investors demand it.

Risk controls: protecting investors from managers

The uncomfortable truth of money management: you're giving a third party control over client funds. Investor-level risk controls are what make that acceptable — maximum drawdown limits per PAMM that pause trading automatically, per-investor stop levels, and transparency requirements on manager strategy and history.

Vet managers like an allocator would: verified track record length, drawdown history (not just returns), strategy description that matches observed trading behavior, and skin in the game — managers with meaningful personal capital in their own PAMM behave differently. Your due diligence process is part of the product; document it and stand behind it.

The operational reality

Money management multiplies your operational surface: onboarding investors with appropriate risk disclosures, handling subscriptions and redemptions on schedule, calculating and distributing performance fees accurately, producing investor statements, and supporting managers with their own reporting needs.

Most of this should be systematized in your PAMM/MAM infrastructure, not handled manually — manual fee calculations across dozens of accounts are where errors (and disputes) breed. And keep compliance in the loop from the start: managed-account offerings attract regulatory attention in most jurisdictions, and the disclosure and licensing requirements vary enormously. Structure first, market second.

Finally, think about the manager pipeline: the best money-management products become talent magnets, attracting skilled traders who bring their own investor followings. Support that flywheel with manager leaderboards, verified performance showcases, and marketing tools managers can use to raise capital. Your PAMM offering stops being a feature and becomes an ecosystem — and ecosystems are very hard for competitors to replicate.

The takeaway

Choose PAMM for simplicity or MAM for flexibility, lock down allocation and high-water-mark fee mechanics in writing, enforce investor-level risk controls with real manager due diligence, and systematize the operations before you scale. Money management is a premium product — it has to feel like one in every detail.
